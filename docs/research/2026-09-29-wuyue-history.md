# Wuyue kingdom (吳越, 907–978) — fact check for a neon short

Sources are pointed to directly (Britannica, Wikipedia sourced from standard histories, ehangzhou.gov.cn museum/tourism pages, one JIABS Buddhist-studies journal — access to its PDF was blocked, so that claim is flagged accordingly). Wikipedia is used mainly as a pointer, cross-checked against Britannica and the JIABS abstract material found via search.

## 1. Founding dates, founder, background, capital

**Verdict: needs change (dates need precision; background confirmed).**

Qian Liu 錢鏐 (852–932) started as a salt-trade smuggler/privateer, then joined a local militia raised against Wang Ying's rebellion (876–877). He rose under warlord Dong Chang, destroyed Dong's forces by 896, and was created Prince of Yue (越王) in 902, then Prince of Wu-Yue (吳越王) in 907 by the founding emperor of Later Liang — this 907 investiture is the conventional founding date of the state. He was raised to King of Wuyue (吳越國王) with full royal privileges in 923. So "907" is right as the state's founding, but the date is an investiture by the Later Liang court, not a unilateral declaration — Qian Liu had already held de facto power in the region since the 890s.

His seat was at Hang Prefecture (杭州), also referred to as Qiantang or Xifu (西府) in some sources — confirm "Xifu" specifically as a period name for the seat rather than a modern gloss; the more consistently attested name is Qiantang.

Corrected wording: Qian Liu (錢鏐, 852–932), a former salt trader turned militia leader, was invested Prince of Yue in 902 and Prince of Wu-Yue in 907 by the Later Liang court; his capital was Hang Prefecture (Hangzhou), then called Qiantang.

**Safe to say on screen:** A salt trader turned soldier built a kingdom around Hangzhou in 907, and it lasted seventy years.

Sources: [Qian Liu — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qian_Liu), [chinaknowledge.de — Wu-Yue](http://www.chinaknowledge.de/History/Tang/rulers-wuyue.html)

## 3. The seawall and the arrow legend

**Verdict: needs change on the date; the arrow story is explicitly legend, not record.**

Qian Liu built a major seawall at the mouth of the Qiantang river and enlarged Hangzhou's city wall around 909 (not 910 — sources converge on 909, though some Chinese municipal pages round to 910). The arrow story — that he ordered thousands of archers to fire on the incoming tidal bore to intimidate the "tide god" before the wall held — is presented in every source as folklore ("is said to have driven back the waters"), attached after the fact to explain how a real, very difficult engineering project succeeded. There's a real, documented seawall; the arrows are legend hung on it.

**Safe to say on screen:** He built a real seawall around 909 — the legend says he fired arrows at the tide first.

Sources: [Qian Liu — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qian_Liu), [King Qian Shoots the Tide — ehangzhou.gov.cn](https://www.ehangzhou.gov.cn/2018-08/16/c_263106.htm)

## 4. Tribute, trade, and Yue ware "secret colour" celadon

**Verdict: confirmed.**

Wuyue paid regular tribute to the Five Dynasties courts and then the Northern Song, while separately keeping direct envoy exchange with the Khitan Liao (mostly by sea) and maritime trade contact with Goryeo, Later Baekje, Balhae, Silla and Japan. Its signature export/tribute good was Yue ware celadon, and specifically a graded "secret colour" (秘色, mise) celadon produced at kilns effectively reserved for the court and for gifts/tribute upward to northern courts. This is well attested archaeologically (Zhejiang kiln sites) and textually.

**Safe to say on screen:** It kept peace by paying tribute north, and grew rich trading a jade-green porcelain so fine it was called "secret colour."

Sources: [Wuyue — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuyue), [Yue ware — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yue_ware)

## 5. Recovering the lost Tiantai texts

**Verdict: core facts confirmed; some specific sequencing is contested by scholars — flag as such on screen.**

Many Tiantai 天台 texts were destroyed or scattered in the An Lushan rebellion (755–763) and, far more severely, the Huichang persecution of Buddhism (845). By the mid-10th century key Tiantai works were effectively lost within China but partially preserved abroad. Qian Chu 錢俶 (929–988) sent an emissary/gifts (gold) toward Japan's Mount Hiei around 953 seeking texts, and in 960/961 sent envoys and fifty gifts to the Goryeo court asking for the missing scriptures. King Gwangjong of Goryeo 光宗 sent the monk Chegwan 諦觀 (d. 970) to China in 961 carrying the requested texts. On arrival, on the advice of the Wuyue Chan/Tiantai patriarch Deshao 德韶, Chegwan sought out the Tiantai master Yiji 義寂 (Luoxi Yiji, 919–987) and studied under him for roughly a decade; Chegwan wrote the introductory manual 天台四教儀 ("Guide to/Outline of the Tiantai Fourfold Teachings"), later influential across Korea, China and Japan.

What's solid: Qian Chu's diplomatic requests to Japan and Goryeo, Gwangjong's dispatch of Chegwan with texts in 961, Chegwan's study under Yiji, and his authorship of 天台四教儀 before his death in 970. What's contested/later-accreted: the exact date Chegwan's texts reached and were catalogued in China (his traditional biography says 962, which later scholarship has questioned), and the precise division of credit between Deshao (who reportedly pointed Chegwan to Yiji) and Yiji (who did the actual teaching) — different accounts emphasize one or the other as the prime mover in Wuyue's Tiantai revival. Treat the Deshao-recommends-Yiji detail as attested but secondary; the primary attested chain is Qian Chu's request → Gwangjong's dispatch → Chegwan studying with Yiji.

**Safe to say on screen:** When Tiantai's own books had been burned at home, the king sent envoys to Korea and Japan to bring them back — and a Korean monk who arrived with them stayed to study, and wrote the book that explained the school to everyone after.

Sources: [Chegwan — Encyclopedia of Buddhism](https://encyclopediaofbuddhism.org/wiki/Chegwan), [Outline of the Tiantai Fourfold Teachings — acmuller.net](http://www.acmuller.net/kor-bud/sagyoui.html) (note: the JIABS paper "Crossing Ten-Thousand Li of Waves: The Return of China's Lost Tiantai Texts" is the primary scholarly source for this episode, but its PDF was blocked from direct fetch during this research — worth reading in full before scripting anything more specific than the above)

## 6. Yongming Yanshou and the 36 Goryeo monks

**Verdict: confirmed.**

Yongming Yanshou 永明延壽 (904–975) was a disciple of the Wuyue Tiantai/Chan patriarch Deshao and became the third patriarch of the Fayan Chan lineage. Qian Chu built Huiri Yongming Temple (later renamed Jingci Temple 淨慈寺) for him in 954. His major work is the 100-fascicle Zongjing lu 宗鏡錄 ("Records of the Source Mirror"), which synthesizes Chan with doctrinal Buddhist schools. King Gwangjong of Goryeo sent gifts and, per the record, monks to study with him; Yanshou is credited with ordaining 36 Korean monks who then returned home to teach.

**Safe to say on screen:** A Goryeo king sent thirty-six monks across the sea just to study with one master in Hangzhou.

Sources: [Yongming Yanshou — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongming_Yanshou), [Jingci Temple — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jingci_Temple)

## 7. The 84,000 stupas and the Baoqieyin dharani sutra

**Verdict: confirmed, with print-date correction.**

Qian Chu, following the legend of the Indian king Ashoka's 84,000 stupas, commissioned a set of 84,000 small stupas (bronze and iron; later brick) distributed for merit and devotion. Each held a printed copy of the Baoqieyin ("Precious Casket Seal") dharani sutra 寶篋印陀羅尼經. Printings are dated 956 (for the bronze stupas), 965 (for the iron stupas), and 975 (associated with a monumental brick pagoda project — that's the Leifeng Pagoda, see below), not 955/965/975 as listed in the claim — the first print run is 956, not 955. These are among the earliest datable printed items surviving, and copies have indeed been recovered in Japan as well as in China (including from Leifeng Pagoda's bricks in 1924).

**Safe to say on screen:** He printed a short sutra by the tens of thousands and sealed one inside each of 84,000 small stupas — some of which turned up centuries later in Japan.

Sources: [What Was in the "Precious Casket Seal"? — MDPI/Religions](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/12/1/13), [History of printing in East Asia — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_typography_in_East_Asia)

## 8. Leifeng Pagoda

**Verdict: mostly confirmed; the "975 vs 977" question resolves to 975 for the start, with completion sometimes dated later.**

Leifeng Pagoda 雷峰塔 was built in 975 under Qian Chu (some sources describe construction running to 977), on the shore of West Lake, reportedly to hold Buddha relic hair and/or in honor of a favored consort, and to pray for the state's peace. It burned down to its brick core in the 16th century (pirate raids/fire) and the bare brick tower collapsed on 25 September 1924 — largely because locals had been pilfering bricks for their supposed medicinal properties. Many bricks held small printed scrolls of the Baoqieyin dharani sutra, recovered at the 1924 collapse. The 2000–2001 Zhejiang Provincial Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology excavation of the underground foundation chamber found 51 groups of objects — reliquaries, Buddhist statues, a gilt silver structure, jade and glass items, and coins spanning 175 BC–960 AD — including a gilt silver stupa-form reliquary. The tower standing today is a 2000–2002 reconstruction, opened 25 October 2002.

**Safe to say on screen:** A thousand-year-old pagoda by West Lake finally fell in 1924, and thirty years ago archaeologists opened its underground vault and found a silver stupa still inside.

Sources: [Leifeng Pagoda — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leifeng_Pagoda), [Leifeng Pagoda (Leifeng ta 雷峰塔) — UBC Open Collections](https://open.library.ubc.ca/media/stream/pdf/52387/1.0423506/4)

## 9. Liuhe, Baochu Pagodas, Lingyin Temple

**Verdict: confirmed, with one date to add.**

Baochu Pagoda was built at West Lake in 963, seven years before Liuhe Pagoda 六和塔 ("Six Harmonies Pagoda"), which was built in 970, reputedly both to calm the Qiantang tidal bore and as a river navigation marker/lighthouse. (Liuhe was destroyed in 1121 and rebuilt by 1165 in the Southern Song — worth knowing so the script doesn't imply the current pagoda is the original 970 structure.) Lingyin Temple was massively expanded under Wuyue patronage, reaching a claimed peak of 18 pavilions, 72 halls, 1,300+ monastic rooms, and over 3,000 monks; the Feilai Feng grottoes' rich rock carvings nearby also largely date to this era.

**Safe to say on screen:** By the 970s the lake was ringed with new pagodas, and the great temple above it housed thousands of monks.

Sources: [Liuhe Pagoda — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liuhe_Pagoda), [Lingyin Temple — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingyin_Temple)

## 10. Peaceful submission to Song, 978

**Verdict: confirmed, with exact date.**

In the fifth month of 978, Qian Chu submitted a memorial at the Song capital Bianjing offering the whole of Wuyue — thirteen prefectures, one military district, eighty-six counties, 550,700+ households, and roughly 115,000 soldiers — to the Song court. Emperor Taizong accepted on the sixth day of that month, receiving the delegation with full court ceremony. This avoided a war for Hangzhou; the transfer is called 納土歸宋 ("returning the land to submit to Song"). Later Qian family tradition frames this as following the ancestral instruction (attributed to Qian Liu) to submit as soon as a legitimate unifying ruler appeared — this "family instruction" (later formalized as 錢氏家訓, the Qian Family Precepts) is a real and famous document/tradition in Chinese family-education history, though the direct causal link ("Qian Chu submitted because of this specific instruction") is the kind of moralized retelling that should be flagged as traditional narrative rather than a contemporary record of Qian Chu's motive.

**Safe to say on screen:** In 978 the last king handed his kingdom to the Song without a fight, and Hangzhou was spared a siege.

Sources: [Qian Chu — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qian_Chu), [Qian family lineage — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qian_family_lineage)

## 11. A good extra visual beat

**Verdict: confirmed and recommend for the script.**

Two candidates, both well attested and visual:

- **West Lake maintenance as state policy.** In 927, Qian Liu formed a standing corps of a thousand soldiers whose job was dredging and clearing weeds from West Lake — an early, deliberate case of a government maintaining a lake as public infrastructure/scenic asset, centuries before it became China's most famous lake.
- **Wuyue as an early printing hub.** The dated 956/965/975 dharani-sutra print runs commissioned by Qian Chu are among the very earliest surviving dated printed texts anywhere, making Wuyue's Hangzhou a real center of the technology roughly five centuries before European printing.

**Safe to say on screen:** He put a standing army to work just keeping the lake clean — and his court printed sutras by the thousand, centuries before Gutenberg.

Sources: [Timeline of Hangzhou — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Hangzhou), [History of typography in East Asia — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_typography_in_East_Asia)
