**1. Facts**

- **“Sixty-four situations.”** Fair as Six Lines’ explanation of the hexagrams. It teaches the reading you follow.
- **“About three thousand years old.”** Reasonable shorthand for the core text’s roots, but the book has layers composed at different times. Exact fix: **“Sixty-four situations, with roots about three thousand years old.”** The Ten Wings came later. [Stanford’s account](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/) explains that distinction.
- **“For most of that time…”** Too sweeping. Your Shaughnessy source supports early use, not a quantified claim about most of three millennia. Philosophical reading also has a long history. Exact fix: **“For centuries, people consulted it about the future.”**
- **“This annoyed a young man called Wang Bi.”** Unsupported, and the connection is misleading. *On Images* criticizes readers who cling to fixed image associations and construct elaborate explanations around them. It does not establish that asking about the future annoyed him. Exact fix: **“Wang Bi offered some reading instructions.”**
- **226–249; “died at twenty-three.”** The dates are standard. Twenty-three is a conventional modern statement, used by [Stanford](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/). But your cited [*Sanguozhi* biography](/Users/auchan/projects/chinese-classics-reference/sanguozhi/text/28-wang-guanqiu-zhuge-deng-zhong-zhuan.md) says twenty-four, consistent with traditional age reckoning. The year dates alone do not establish his exact birthday age. Cleanest fix: **“He died young.”** Keep 226–249 on screen.
- **“The commentary China read the book through for centuries.”** Substantially grounded, but “China” implies everyone and “the commentary” implies exclusivity. His commentary became an official standard through Kong Yingda’s edition; other readings existed. Exact fix: **“His commentary became a standard for centuries.”** [Stanford](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/) supports its official standing.
- **“Stop staring at the pictures.”** Funny, but it skips the necessary first step: use the image to understand the meaning. Exact fix: **“Grasp the meaning. Then forget the image.”** Also, “images” here includes textual images and symbolic associations, not merely illustrations.
- **The fish trap.** Wang Bi uses it, but does not originate it. The earlier analogy appears in [*Zhuangzi*, chapter 26](/Users/auchan/projects/chinese-classics-reference/zhuangzi/text/26-wai-wu.md), concerning words and meaning. Wang Bi applies it to images and meaning. “A picture, he said…” is defensible attribution of use; give Zhuangzi credit to avoid implying invention.
- **“If the meaning is strength, what do you need the horse for?”** A fair, lively paraphrase of 義苟在健，何必馬乎. 健 carries vigor or strong activity; “strength” is acceptable here. **“If the meaning is strength, why insist on a horse?”** more clearly expresses his objection to treating the horse as necessary.
- **“The fish…is judgment.”** This is Six Lines’ application, not Wang Bi’s literal identification. His fish is meaning generally. Exact fix: **“Six Lines reads it for judgment: what makes sense to do here.”**
- **The recommendation footer.** Keep it. Present it as the device’s recommendation for this reading, rather than a historical claim about every use of the book.

Two picture corrections: yarrow stalks should be **sorted and counted**, not shaken from a pot; and the cart losing its spokes belongs to **hexagram 9, Small Taming**, not Qian. Keep the montage, but remove the implication that all three images occur in Qian.

**2. Humor and length**

It is mildly funny in the intended dry register. The device calmly issuing reading instructions is enough of a comic frame. Let the animation carry some of the humor.

The weakest lines are:

- **“This annoyed…”** supplies a familiar comic personality at the expense of accuracy.
- **The commentary sentence:** 18 words of biography and historical importance; too much before the lesson.
- **The fish-trap beat:** 26 words explains the mechanism three times.
- **“The fish, in this case…”** adds a 19-word explanation after the explanation. “A sensible person” also sounds slightly patronizing.

The draft has **131 words**, counting hyphenated compounds as one; **133 spoken words** if their parts are counted separately. At 2.3 words a second, that is **about 58 seconds before pauses**. Expect over a minute with a measured read.

The revision below has **92 written words / 93 spoken words**: about **40 seconds of speech**, leaving roughly five seconds for pauses.

**4. The voice**

**Pick another.** There is a real reputational risk for a brand account.

Attenborough publicly objected to AI copies of his voice in November 2024. [BBC News reported his response directly](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-72XVvwZcfI). The supplied description—“David,” “national treasure,” “classic nature narrator”—suggests that association. That is an inference from the marketing, not proof that this particular voice copies him. I have not auditioned it or verified its provenance.

Directing the read away from documentary cadence may reduce resemblance, but cannot reliably remove a recognizable vocal identity. An AI label explains how the audio was made; it does not establish consent. ElevenLabs’ [use policy](https://elevenlabs.io/use-policy) also restricts intentional replication without consent or legal right.

Choose a voice with documented consent or an original synthetic voice that is not recognizable as a public figure. Direction: **plain, even pace; short pauses; no wonder, reverence, or emphasis announcing the jokes.**

Keep your own device design, interface, and wording. The draft already excludes the obvious franchise phrases and props. There is no need to add a more elaborate Adams-like sentence: it would lengthen the short and weaken the plain voice.
